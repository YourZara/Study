Ch 8. DC Machine

5 hours · 5 marks

8.1 Constructional Details

A DC machine (motor or generator) has the following main parts:

PartFunction / notes
Yoke (frame)Outer cast-iron/steel casing; mechanical support; carries magnetic flux of the field system.
Poles & pole shoesField poles bolted to yoke; laminated shoes spread flux and support field winding.
Field windingCopper coils on poles; produce main magnetic field when excited.
Armature coreLaminated cylindrical iron; slots hold armature conductors; rotates in magnetic field.
Armature windingLap or wave winding in slots; where EMF is induced / torque produced.
CommutatorCylinder of copper segments insulated by mica; mechanical rectifier/inverter — converts AC in armature to DC at brushes (generator) or DC supply to alternating currents in coils (motor).
BrushesCarbon/graphite; press on commutator; connect rotating armature circuit to external stationary circuit.
Shaft, bearings, interpolesMechanical support; interpoles (compoles) improve commutation under load.

Commutator and brushes (exam focus)

The armature winding carries alternating current relative to the rotating conductors. The commutator + brushes form a switching arrangement that:

  • As generator: rectifies the induced AC so that brush polarity is unidirectional (DC output).
  • As motor: reverses current in each coil as it passes under a brush so that torque remains unidirectional.

Good commutation requires brush at magnetic neutral axis (or slightly shifted / with interpoles). Sparking indicates poor commutation (high reactance voltage, worn brushes, wrong brush position).

8.2 Generator Principle & EMF Equation

Principle: Faraday’s law — when armature conductors cut magnetic flux (driven by prime mover), an EMF is induced. Commutator delivers DC at the brushes.

Average EMF per conductor $= B\ell v$. For $Z$ conductors, $P$ poles, $A$ parallel paths, flux/pole $\Phi$, speed $N$ rpm:

$$E_g = \frac{\Phi Z N}{60}\times\frac{P}{A}$$
  • Lap winding: $A = P$
  • Wave winding: $A = 2$

Also $E_g = V + I_a R_a$ (+ brush drop if considered) for a separately/shunt generator supplying terminal voltage $V$.

8.3 Motor Principle & Torque Equation

Principle: When a current-carrying conductor lies in a magnetic field, it experiences a force $F=B I \ell$ (Fleming’s left-hand rule). Forces on armature conductors produce a torque about the shaft.

Production of torque

Under a N-pole, conductors carrying current into the page experience force in one tangential direction; under S-pole, reverse current (via commutator) experiences force in the same rotational sense → continuous unidirectional torque.

Gross (developed) torque: electrical power converted $= E_b I_a = T\omega$, with $\omega=2\pi N/60$:

$$T = \frac{E_b I_a}{\omega} = 0.159\,\Phi Z I_a\frac{P}{A}\quad(\mathrm{N\cdot m})$$

In proportional form: $T \propto \Phi I_a$. For constant field (shunt), $T \propto I_a$.

8.5 Back EMF and Its Role

When a DC motor armature rotates in the field, an EMF is induced that opposes the applied voltage — the back EMF $E_b$:

$$E_b = \frac{\Phi Z N}{60}\frac{P}{A} = V - I_a R_a$$

Roles of back EMF:

  • Makes the motor self-regulating: if load ↑ → speed dips slightly → $E_b$ ↓ → $I_a=(V-E_b)/R_a$ ↑ → torque ↑ to meet load.
  • Limits armature current in running condition ($R_a$ is small; without $E_b$, $I_a\approx V/R_a$ would be huge).
  • At start, $N=0$ → $E_b=0$ → starting current $=V/R_a$ is dangerously high → need a starter.

8.6 Performance Characteristics of DC Motor

  • Torque–current: shunt $T\propto I_a$ (straight line); series $T\propto I_a^2$ (unsaturated).
  • Speed–current / speed–torque: shunt — almost flat (slight droop); series — speed high at light load, falls as torque rises.
  • Speed–torque: shunt suitable for constant-speed drives; series for traction, cranes (high $T_{\mathrm{st}}$).

8.7 Starting with 3-Point Starter

At start $E_b=0$, so a variable resistance (starter) is inserted in series with the armature to limit $I_{\mathrm{st}}$ to a safe value (≈1.5–2.5× FL).

Three-point starter (shunt motor) terminals: L (line), A (armature), F (field).

  • Starting resistance graded; handle moves from OFF toward RUN, cutting out resistance stepwise.
  • No-volt release (NVR) coil in series with field — holds handle in RUN; if supply fails or field opens, spring returns handle to OFF (protects against restart on full voltage / runaway on field failure).
  • Overload release (OLR) — electromagnet across a shunt; heavy current attracts armature shorting NVR → handle returns to OFF.

“Three-point” = L, A, F connection points. (Four-point starter puts NVR across supply for better field-control compatibility.)

8.8 Speed Control (Armature, Field)

From $N \propto E_b/\Phi \approx (V - I_a R_a)/\Phi$:
MethodHowEffect
Armature voltage / rheostatSeries resistance with armature, or variable $V_a$Speed below rated (rheostat); Ward–Leonard / chopper for wide range. Rheostat: inefficient ($I_a^2 R$ loss).
Field controlRheostat in shunt-field circuit; weaken $\Phi$Speed above rated; limited by commutation and mechanical strength.

8.9 Losses and Efficiency

  • Copper losses: $I_a^2 R_a$, $I_{\mathrm{sh}}^2 R_{\mathrm{sh}}$, series-field $I_{\mathrm{se}}^2 R_{\mathrm{se}}$.
  • Iron losses: hysteresis + eddy in armature core (and poles).
  • Mechanical: friction + windage.
  • Stray / brush contact losses.

Iron + friction + windage often grouped as constant losses.

$$\eta_{\mathrm{commercial}} = \frac{\text{output}}{\text{input}} = \frac{\text{output}}{\text{output}+\text{total losses}}$$

For generator: output $= V I_L$; input = mechanical power from prime mover ≈ $E_g I_a$ (gross electrical generated).

Solved Examples

Solved Example 1 — Shunt generator: $E_g$ and commercial efficiency

Q. A shunt generator delivers 96 A at 200 V. Armature resistance $R_a=0.1\,\Omega$, shunt-field resistance $R_{\mathrm{sh}}=50\,\Omega$. Iron and friction losses are 2500 W. Find the generated EMF and commercial efficiency.

Shunt current:

$$I_{\mathrm{sh}} = \frac{V}{R_{\mathrm{sh}}} = \frac{200}{50} = 4\,\mathrm{A}$$

Armature current (generator: armature supplies load + shunt):

$$I_a = I_L + I_{\mathrm{sh}} = 96 + 4 = 100\,\mathrm{A}$$

Generated EMF:

$$E_g = V + I_a R_a = 200 + 100\times 0.1 = \mathbf{210\,\mathrm{V}}$$

Output $= V I_L = 200\times 96 = 19200\,\mathrm{W}$

Copper losses:

$$I_a^2 R_a + I_{\mathrm{sh}}^2 R_{\mathrm{sh}} = 100^2\times 0.1 + 4^2\times 50 = 1000 + 800 = 1800\,\mathrm{W}$$

Iron + friction $= 2500\,\mathrm{W}$

Total losses $= 1800 + 2500 = 4300\,\mathrm{W}$

Input $= \text{output} + \text{losses} = 19200 + 4300 = 23500\,\mathrm{W}$

$$\eta_{\mathrm{comm}} = \frac{19200}{23500}\times 100\% = \mathbf{81.70\%}$$

(Check: generated power $E_g I_a = 210\times 100 = 21000\,\mathrm{W}$; after Cu losses in armature+field path accounting, commercial $\eta$ uses terminal output / mechanical input.)

Solved Example 2 — Shunt motor speed after armature series resistance & torque drop

Q. A 240 V shunt motor has $R_a=0.4\,\Omega$ and $R_f=120\,\Omega$. It draws 30 A at half load and runs at 1400 rpm. A resistance of $1.2\,\Omega$ is connected in series with the armature and the torque is reduced by 20%. Find the new speed. (Field unchanged.)

Field current: $I_f = V/R_f = 240/120 = 2\,\mathrm{A}$

Half-load armature current: $I_{a1} = 30 - 2 = 28\,\mathrm{A}$

$$E_{b1} = V - I_{a1} R_a = 240 - 28\times 0.4 = 240 - 11.2 = 228.8\,\mathrm{V}$$

Since $T\propto\Phi I_a$ and $\Phi$ constant (shunt field unchanged):

$$T_2 = 0.8\,T_1 \;\Rightarrow\; I_{a2} = 0.8\times 28 = 22.4\,\mathrm{A}$$

With $1.2\,\Omega$ in series with armature:

$$E_{b2} = V - I_{a2}(R_a+1.2) = 240 - 22.4\times(0.4+1.2) = 240 - 22.4\times 1.6 = 240 - 35.84 = 204.16\,\mathrm{V}$$

$N\propto E_b/\Phi$; $\Phi$ constant:

$$N_2 = N_1\times\frac{E_{b2}}{E_{b1}} = 1400\times\frac{204.16}{228.8} = \mathbf{1249\,\mathrm{rpm}}\text{ (approx.)}$$
Theory Commutator, brushes & torque production

Commutator: segmented copper cylinder; converts generated AC in coils to DC at brushes (generator), and ensures torque remains unidirectional (motor) by reversing coil current under brush.

Brushes: carbon blocks sliding on commutator; connect external circuit; placed near GNA for sparkless commutation.

Torque: $F=BI\ell$ on each conductor; commutator maintains correct current direction under N and S poles so all forces contribute to the same rotation; $T=0.159\,\Phi Z I_a (P/A)$.

Yoke, poles, field winding, armature core & winding, commutator, brushes — see §8.1.

$E_g=\Phi Z N P/(60 A)$. State $A=P$ (lap), $A=2$ (wave). See §8.2.

$F=BI\ell$; $E_b I_a=T\omega$; $T\propto\Phi I_a$. Commutator keeps unidirectional torque. See §8.3.

$E_b=V-I_a R_a$; self-regulates $I_a$; at start $E_b=0$ so $I_{\mathrm{st}}=V/R_a$ is very large. See §8.5.
Q5. Explain the working of a 3-point starter for a DC shunt motor. 5 marks
Graded resistance; NVR in field; OLR protection; L–A–F connections. See §8.7.
Q6. Explain armature and field methods of speed control of a DC shunt motor. 4 marks
Armature: speed below base; field weakening: above base. See §8.8.

Q9. Classify DC machines and state applications of series and shunt motors. 3 marks
